\title{\vspace{-1.6em}How Certain Is the Chatbot Arena Leaderboard?\\[0.2em]
\large Multiplicity, Rank Spreads and Category Specialties}
\author{Harry Hu \and Chloe Yu \and Ender Yang \and Chenxi Ge}
\date{\vspace{-0.4em}STAT 236 Final Project, Fall 2026}

\begin{document}

\begin{abstract}
\noindent Chatbot Arena ranks language models with a Bradley--Terry model fit to pairwise human votes and shows each model's \emph{rank spread}, the ranks consistent with per-model 95\% rating intervals. We verify that these intervals carry no multiplicity correction, in 2024 and on today's site. In a semi-synthetic simulation on Arena's real comparison graph, all 129 rank spreads hold simultaneously in 90.9\% of datasets, against 0.3\% for the rating intervals. Every correction we study restores simultaneous coverage; pairwise $z$-tests with Holm's step-down procedure give the narrowest valid rank intervals of the seven procedures we simulate (11.3 ranks, against 13.6 for Bonferroni and 33--43 for the $\chi^2$ set of the original Arena paper) and remain valid when many models are exactly or nearly tied. On Arena's category leaderboards, Higher Criticism, max-$|z|$ and $\chi^2$ tests all reject the null of no specialties, and Bonferroni flags 275 of 958 (model, category) pairs, a count that depends on the calibration of the null's noise level.
\end{abstract}

%====================================================================
\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Chatbot Arena, now run by LMArena at arena.ai, is the most widely cited leaderboard for large language models. Users vote for the better of two anonymous answers, and the site ranks the models by a Bradley--Terry (BT) fit \citep{bradley1952rank} to these votes. Since 2025 it has also reported each model's \emph{rank spread}, the best and worst rank consistent with all models' rating intervals \citep{lmarena2025ranking}, which a reader naturally takes as a confidence statement.

The paper behind the site \citep{chiang2024arena} notes that a ranking depends on all ratings at once and therefore needs intervals that hold simultaneously, and it proposes a $\chi^2$ confidence set for this purpose. The current open-source code \citep{lmarena2025arenarank} instead computes per-model 95\% intervals, $\hat r_m \pm 1.96\,\SE_m$. This introduces two departures from the nominal level that act in opposite directions. Without a multiplicity correction, about 5\% of the intervals fail to cover (about 6 of 129 models in 2024 and 20 of today's roughly 400). But the rank spread separates two models only when their intervals do not overlap, which for two independent estimates with equal standard errors is a test at $|z| > 1.96\sqrt2 = 2.77$, the 99.4\% level. Their combined effect on simultaneous coverage has not, to our knowledge, been measured. Arena also publishes category leaderboards (coding, math, Chinese and others). We call a \emph{specialty} a (model, category) pair in which the model is stronger or weaker than its overall rating predicts; with about a thousand such pairs, some apparent specialties will arise by chance.

We ask three questions. \textbf{Q1:} How often does Arena's rank spread contain the true rank, model by model and for all models at once? \textbf{Q2:} Which multiplicity-corrected procedures restore 95\% simultaneous coverage, and how much wider are their rank intervals? \textbf{Q3:} Do category specialties exist, and if so, which pairs have them? Because true ranks are unknown on real data, Q1 and Q2 use a semi-synthetic simulation in which the BT fit on the real 2024 battles is the truth and only the outcomes are redrawn. For Q3 we calibrate specialty statistics by a parametric bootstrap and compare Higher Criticism \citep{donoho2004higher} with the maximum-$|z|$ (min-$P$) and $\chi^2$ tests under the sparse-signal (Rare/Weak) model \citep{donoho2015higher}.

Related work on inference for ranks includes bootstrap intervals \citep{hall2009using}, joint confidence regions for a ranking \citep{klein2020joint}, and simultaneous confidence sets built from pairwise differences with step-down testing \citep{mogstad2024inference}; our pairwise Holm procedure is the Bonferroni--Holm version of the last. We evaluate these ideas on Arena's own data and comparison graph.

\section{Data and Arena's method}\label{sec:data}

\paragraph{Data.} We use the public battle dump \citep{lmsys2024dump} released with \citet{chiang2024arena} and, as an answer key, the official leaderboard snapshot \texttt{elo\_results\_20240813} \citep[last updated \mbox{2024-08-12};][]{lmarena2026dataset}. The official filter (anonymous battles, the de-duplicated subsample, battles up to the snapshot time) leaves 1,657,517 battles among $M = 129$ models. Only 3,457 of the $K = \binom{129}{2} = 8{,}256$ pairs ever met, and 34.5\% of battles are ties. Unlike the 2025 sample named in our proposal, this dump comes with an answer key computed by Arena from the same battles. For the current site we use the 2026 boards in \texttt{leaderboard-dataset} (downloaded 2026-10-28) and our record of the top 59 models that arena.ai displayed on 2026-10-08.

\paragraph{Model.} Code a battle between models $i$ and $j$ as $y \in \{1, \tfrac12, 0\}$ (win, tie, loss for $i$) and write $\sigma(x) = 1/(1 + e^{-x})$. Arena's \texttt{arena-rank} package estimates the strengths $\beta$ by minimizing the logistic loss with fractional outcomes,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:loss}
\hb = \arg\min_{\beta}\; -\sum_{\text{battles}} \bigl[\,y\,(\beta_i - \beta_j) - \log\bigl(1 + e^{\beta_i - \beta_j}\bigr)\bigr],
\end{equation}
whose population minimizer satisfies $\mathbb E[y] = \sigma(\beta_i - \beta_j)$. It reports ratings $\hat r_m = 1000 + (400/\ln 10)\,\hb_m$. The covariance of $\hb$ is estimated by the sandwich $\hat V = \hat H^{-1}\hat G\hat H^{-1}$ \citep{white1980heteroskedasticity}, with $\hat H$ the Hessian of \eqref{eq:loss} and $\hat G$ the covariance of the per-battle gradients; below, $\hat V$ is on the rating scale. Arena's rating intervals are $[L_m, U_m] = \hat r_m \pm 1.96\,\SE_m$ with $\SE_m = \hat V_{mm}^{1/2}$, and the rank spread is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:spread}
\text{best}_m = 1 + \#\{m' : L_{m'} > U_m\}, \qquad \text{worst}_m = 1 + \#\{m' \neq m : U_{m'} > L_m\}.
\end{equation}
We reserve \emph{rank spread} for this construction and call the output of any procedure a \emph{rank interval}. Its width is $\text{worst}_m - \text{best}_m + 1$, so a model with a single rank has width 1.

\paragraph{Validation against the published boards.} Refitting the public battles with \texttt{arena-rank} reproduces the 2024 ratings to a median of 0.06 points, and the 2024 intervals have a half-width of 1.90 bootstrap SDs, as per-model 95\% intervals should (Bonferroni would need 3.55). Every interval on the 4,773 published 2026 text-board snapshots is $\hat r \pm 1.9600\,\SE$, and \eqref{eq:spread} reproduces the site's rank spreads for all 59 recorded models (Appendix~\ref{app:step0}). In 2024 as now, the published intervals carry no multiplicity correction, and the published ranks are built from them.

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Semi-synthetic simulation}\label{sec:sim}

We fit \eqref{eq:loss} once on the real battles, treat the fitted strengths $\beta^*$ as the truth, and define the true ranks $R_m = 1 + \#\{m' : \beta^*_{m'} > \beta^*_m\}$ (tied models share the best rank of their group). Each simulated dataset keeps the same 3,457 pairs with their real battle counts and redraws the outcomes. With $p = \sigma(\beta^*_i - \beta^*_j)$ and the observed tie rate $t = 0.345$, a battle is a win, tie or loss for $i$ with probabilities $p - t/2$, $t$ and $1 - p - t/2$, so that $\mathbb E[y] = p$ and $\beta^*$ is exactly the estimand of \eqref{eq:loss}. We refit, apply every procedure to the same fit, and repeat 1,000 times. We record per-model and simultaneous coverage, mean width, and \emph{false orderings} (``$i$ above $j$'' declared although $\beta^*_i \le \beta^*_j$), each with a Monte Carlo SE (Appendix~\ref{app:impl}).

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:orderings}
Let a procedure declare a set $D$ of orderings ``$i$ above $j$'', and let $\text{best}_m = 1 + \#\{i : (i, m) \in D\}$ and $\text{worst}_m = M - \#\{j : (m, j) \in D\}$. For $D = \{(i, j) : L_i > U_j\}$ this is the rank spread \eqref{eq:spread}. If $D$ contains no false ordering, then $\text{best}_m \le R_m \le \text{worst}_m$ for every $m$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
If all declared orderings are true, the models declared above $m$ are among the $R_m - 1$ models truly above it, and those declared below $m$ are among the at most $M - R_m$ models truly below it.
\end{proof}

Hence any procedure whose probability of a false ordering is at most 5\% has simultaneous rank coverage of at least 95\% (cf.\ \citealp{mogstad2024inference}). A false ordering needs two models of nearly equal strength.

\subsection{Corrected procedures (Q2)}\label{sec:procs}

\Cref{tab:procs} lists when each procedure declares $i$ above $j$. Bonferroni and the $\chi^2$ set widen the intervals and keep the overlap rule. The $\chi^2$ set of \citet{chiang2024arena},
\[
\{\beta : (\hb - \beta)^\top\hat V^{-}(\hb - \beta) \le \chi^2_{M-1,\,0.95}\},
\]
is used either projected onto each coordinate or through pairwise contrasts \citep{scheffe1953method}. The pairwise procedures test each pair directly with
\[
z_{ij} = \frac{\hat r_i - \hat r_j}{\SE(\hat r_i - \hat r_j)}, \qquad \SE(\hat r_i - \hat r_j)^2 = \hat V_{ii} + \hat V_{jj} - 2\hat V_{ij}.
\]
In these units the overlap rule requires $|z_{ij}| > 1.96\,\rho_{ij}$ with $\rho_{ij} = (\SE_i + \SE_j)/\SE(\hat r_i - \hat r_j)$. On the real fit $\rho_{ij}$ ranges from 1.09 to 1.73 (median 1.37); it exceeds $\sqrt2$ only for the 20\% of pairs whose estimates are clearly positively correlated, typically models that play each other often or share many opponents. \emph{Pairwise Bonferroni} uses the critical value $z_{1-\alpha/(2K)} = 4.52$ for all pairs. \emph{Holm}'s procedure \citep{holm1979simple} compares the $k$-th largest $|z|$ with $c_k = z_{1-\alpha/(2(K-k+1))}$, stops at the first pair that fails, and declares each rejected pair in the direction of $z_{ij}$. \emph{One-sided Holm} applies Holm's procedure to the $2K$ hypotheses $H_{ij}: \beta_i \le \beta_j$, whose rejection is exactly the ordering ``$i$ above $j$''; its $k$-th critical value is $z_{1-\alpha/(2K-k+1)}$.

\paragraph{Guarantees.} By \cref{lem:orderings} the quantity to control is the probability of any false ordering. Bonferroni-type rules bound it by the union bound. Holm's theorem bounds the probability of rejecting any \emph{true} hypothesis, and for one-sided Holm the true hypotheses are exactly the false orderings. For Holm, an exactly tied pair is a true null and is covered by the theorem, but a pair that is close and not tied is a false null, and its possible error is a wrong \emph{direction}. Directional control after a two-sided Holm procedure is proven for independent normal statistics \citep{shaffer1980control}, but our $z_{ij}$ are dependent because pairs share models, and for dependent statistics only special structures are covered \citep{finner1999stepwise, guo2016directional}; we test it by simulation. The proven one-sided version gains little from stepping down: at most one of $H_{ij}$ and $H_{ji}$ can be rejected, so at step $k \le K$ at least $K + 1$ hypotheses remain and its critical value never falls below $z_{1-\alpha/(K+1)} = 4.38$. The declared orderings of all three pairwise procedures are always transitive (Appendix~\ref{app:q2}).

\paragraph{Benchmark and stress tests.} As an oracle benchmark we recompute rank spreads from $\hb \pm z\,\SE$ on a grid of $z$ from 1.8 to 4 and find the smallest $z$ that reaches 95\% simultaneous coverage; it uses the true ranks, so it is not a procedure. To test the guarantees where they bind, we rerun the simulation on the same graph with all strengths scaled by 0.5, 0.25, 0.1 or 0; with the models sorted into 20 or 10 equal-size tiers of exactly equal strength (354 or 768 tied pairs); and with 20 tiers in which each model keeps 5\%, 10\% or 20\% of its distance from the tier mean (median within-tier gaps of 0.1--0.4 SEs), where every false ordering is a wrong-direction error not covered by Holm's theorem.

\subsection{Category specialties (Q3)}\label{sec:q3methods}

We define eight categories from the dump's prompt tags (coding, math, instruction following, hard prompts, Chinese, Russian, multi-turn, long query). Each definition reproduces the official 2024 board of the same name (Appendix~\ref{app:q3}). We test the $N = 958$ (model, category) pairs with at least 200 battles in the category. For model $m$ in category $c$, let $y_b$ be its score in battle $b$ and $p_b = \sigma(\hb_m - \hb_{o(b)})$ the win probability implied by the \emph{overall} fit against opponent $o(b)$. The specialty statistic is
\[
z_{mc} = \textstyle\sum_b (y_b - p_b) \big/ \sqrt{\sum_b (y_b - p_b)^2},
\]
summed over $m$'s battles in $c$ (the empirical variance accounts for ties). The overall fit uses these battles too, so $z_{mc}$ is shrunk toward zero and the $z_{mc}$ are correlated. We therefore calibrate by a parametric bootstrap under the null of no specialties: redraw all outcomes from the overall fit and refit, 500 times. Under the null the raw $z_{mc}$ have standard deviations from 0.70 to 1.05. Each $z_{mc}$ is standardized against its own null mean and SD to $z^*_{mc}$, with two-sided $p$-values from $N(0,1)$.

To test whether \emph{any} specialty exists we use Higher Criticism,
\[
\mathrm{HC} = \max_{k \le N/2} \frac{\sqrt N\,(k/N - p_{(k)})}{\sqrt{p_{(k)}(1 - p_{(k)})}},
\]
its variant HC$^+$ restricted to $p_{(k)} > 1/N$, the maximum-$|z^*|$ test (min-$P$) and $\chi^2 = \sum z^{*2}$, each with its $p$-value from the null draws. To find \emph{which} pairs, we use Bonferroni at family-wise level 5\% and Benjamini--Hochberg \citep{benjamini1995controlling} at false discovery rate 10\%. For power, we plant $s = N^{1-\vartheta}$ specialties with random signs at random pairs, for $\vartheta \in \{0.2, 0.3, \dots, 0.7\}$, with strength set so that each planted $z$ has mean $\mu = \sqrt{2r\log N}$ before the refit, for $r \in \{0.1, 0.2, 0.4, 0.8, 1.5\}$; 200 replicates per cell, with critical values from fresh null draws.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Q1: Coverage of Arena's rank spreads}\label{sec:q1}


Each rating interval covers its true rating 94.8\% of the time, but on average 6.65 of the 129 fail to cover, and all 129 cover in only 3 of 1,000 datasets (\cref{fig:q1}a). The rank spreads cover far more often: each contains its true rank 99.9\% of the time, and all 129 do so in 90.9\% of datasets (Monte Carlo SE 0.9\%). The shortfall from 95\% is small but well beyond Monte Carlo error (4.5 SEs).

By \cref{lem:orderings} a rank spread fails only through a false ordering, and the overlap rule's conservatism absorbs most of the rating intervals that fail to cover, so the two effects nearly cancel. The rank spread is thus very conservative for each model and slightly anti-conservative jointly. Its simultaneous coverage barely changes when all strengths are scaled down (90.1--91.5\% at scales 0.5 to 0.1, which act like up to 100 times less data), but it collapses once models are exactly or nearly tied (\cref{sec:q2}). Across models, spread width is most strongly associated with crowding, the number of models within 10 rating points (Spearman correlation 0.79; \cref{fig:q1}b). Given crowding, the standard error also matters (OLS $t = 8.9$), but the true rank does not ($t = 1.1$; Appendix~\ref{app:q1}).

Every correction reaches 100\% simultaneous coverage without a single false ordering, but the widths differ by a factor of four (\cref{fig:q2}a). The $\chi^2$ set is by far the most expensive: 33.1 ranks read through contrasts and 43.4 projected. It protects every linear combination of the 129 strengths, and its critical value $(\chi^2_{128,\,0.95})^{1/2} = 12.47$ grows like $\sqrt M$, whereas a ranking depends only on the signs of $K$ differences, whose Bonferroni critical value grows only like $\sqrt{2\log K}$. Bonferroni intervals need 13.6 ranks. Holm needs 11.3, 18\% less, and it detects 92.0\% of the true orderings against Bonferroni's 90.1\%.

The gain has two sources. Testing each difference directly saves 0.9 ranks (13.6 to 12.7), because the overlap rule effectively multiplies the critical value by $\rho_{ij} \approx 1.37$ ($3.55 \times 1.37 \approx 4.86 > 4.52$). Holm's step-down saves 1.5 ranks more: about 7,600 pairs are rejected, so only about 660 remain, and the critical value falls from 4.52 to $z_{1-\alpha/(2 \cdot 660)} = 3.96$. One-sided Holm reaches only 12.4 ranks, because its critical value cannot fall below 4.38. All corrections still overshoot. The oracle reaches 95.6\% coverage at $z = 2.1$ with 8.5 ranks.

\paragraph{Stress tests.} A pair whose true gap is $\delta$ standard errors is declared in the wrong direction with probability about $\Phi(-\delta - c)$, where $c$ is its critical value, so errors come from near-ties, which the 2024 strengths rarely have. \Cref{fig:q2}b shows the worlds where family-wise bounds bind. With 20 or 10 exactly tied tiers, Holm's critical value falls to 3.96 or 4.01 and Holm declares a false ordering in 3.1\% and 3.4\% of datasets (Monte Carlo SE 0.5\%), close to the union-bound approximations $354 \times 2\Phi(-3.96) \approx 2.7\%$ and $768 \times 2\Phi(-4.01) \approx 4.6\%$; the other pairwise procedures stay at or below 0.5\%. With all models tied, all three coincide at 2.7\%.

In the near-tie worlds, where Holm has no proven guarantee, its error rate is 1.7\% at a 5\% spread and 0.4--0.6\% at larger spreads, with simultaneous coverage of at least 99.4\%. Exact ties, which Holm's theorem covers, are thus the hardest case, and once they exist Holm's extra width nearly disappears: the oracle needs 10.7 ranks with 20 tied tiers (Holm 11.3) and 13.8 with 10 tiers (Holm 14.0). Arena's uncorrected spreads all hold in only 24\%, 7\% and 0.2\% of datasets with 20 tiers, 10 tiers and all models tied (Appendix~\ref{app:q2}).

\paragraph{The real 2024 board.} Holm keeps the top three models at single ranks, as Arena does, whereas Bonferroni ties the top two (\cref{fig:real}); mean widths are 7.9 ranks for Arena, 11.4 for Holm and 13.8 for Bonferroni. Of the 7,808 orderings Arena declares, Holm does not support 223, whose $|z|$ lie between 2.5 and 3.96: each is significant on its own, but none survives the correction for 8,256 comparisons. Conversely, Holm separates none of the 448 pairs whose rating intervals overlap, because Arena's implicit critical value $1.96\,\rho_{ij}$ is at most 3.4. Holm's rank intervals also separate none of the 830 pairs whose rank spreads overlap (the site's tie rule); unlike the previous result, this one is empirical.

The 2024 category effects are far from the null (\cref{fig:q3}a, \cref{tab:q3}). Every global test rejects at the smallest attainable $p$-value. A naive cut at $p < 0.05$ would flag 521 pairs where 48 are expected by chance. Bonferroni flags 275 pairs, in every category (17--40\% of the pairs tested in each) and for 94 of the 129 models, and the median $|$effect$|$ of the flagged pairs is 25 rating points. BH flags 525, slightly more than the naive cut, because its cutoff $0.1 \times 525/958$ exceeds 0.05. The largest effects concern language: \texttt{llama-3-70b-instruct} is 73 points weaker in Chinese than overall ($z^* = -40$), and all nine Qwen models are 50--84 points stronger in Chinese than overall (eight pass Bonferroni, all nine pass BH).

The number of specialties is much less certain than their existence. The null ignores correlated votes (same user or session, clusters in time) and any misfit of the BT model, and the robust SD of the observed $z^*$ is 3.3 rather than 1 (an overstatement of the noise, since it includes the true specialties). If the true noise were 1.5, 2 or 3 times the null's, Bonferroni would flag 149, 76 or 26 pairs instead of 275.

The power simulation (\cref{fig:q3}b,c) reproduces the qualitative predictions of the Rare/Weak model, in which $s = N^{1-\vartheta}$ signals of size $\mu$ are detectable only above a boundary in $(\vartheta, r)$. With 31 planted specialties ($\vartheta = 0.5$) at $r = 0.4$, HC and HC$^+$ have power 0.88 and 0.95 against 0.56 for min-$P$, and with 122 ($\vartheta = 0.3$) at $r = 0.2$ min-$P$ reaches only 0.57 while $\chi^2$ and HC$^+$ reach 1.00. With 8 ($\vartheta = 0.7$) the ordering reverses as the theory predicts: at $r = 0.8$ HC has power 0.85 against 0.74 for min-$P$ and 0.62 for $\chi^2$, about five Monte Carlo SEs apart. (For $\vartheta < 1/2$ the usual calibration lets the signal shrink with $N$; with fixed $\mu = \sqrt{2r\log N}$ every test is asymptotically powerful there, so the differences at small $\vartheta$ are finite-$N$ effects.) For identification, BH finds 76--93\% of the strongest planted specialties ($r = 1.5$, about 27 rating points at the median pair) and Bonferroni about 53\%; in between lies a wide region where $\chi^2$ and HC$^+$ detect specialties that no procedure can locate. The real board is far from that region, with global statistics one to two orders of magnitude above their null 95\% points.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

Arena's rank spreads hold simultaneously in 91\% of simulated datasets because two effects offset each other, and they fail badly when models are tied; Holm restores simultaneous coverage at the lowest cost among the procedures we simulated, and its extra width buys protection against exact ties. Category specialties are widespread, but their number depends on the null's noise level.

\paragraph{Interpretation.} Three points go beyond this dataset. First, the object that needs a multiplicity guarantee is the set of declared orderings, not the vector of strengths (\cref{lem:orderings}); the $\chi^2$ set spends most of its width on directions that cannot change a ranking. Second, the overlap rule hides a test at $|z| > 1.96\,\rho_{ij}$ with $\rho_{ij}$ near 1.4, which is why Arena's uncorrected spreads are nearly valid and why no correction separates a pair whose rating intervals overlap. Third, the error rate of a pairwise procedure is roughly the number of nearly tied pairs times the tail probability beyond their critical value. That critical value drops only as well-separated pairs are rejected, so tiers of tied models among well-separated ones are the worst case. Resampling the joint distribution of the $z_{ij}$ \citep{romano2005exact, mogstad2024inference} or using the logical constraints among pairwise hypotheses \citep{shaffer1986modified} can only lower Holm's critical values; on the real board the first adds just 18 orderings. A procedure that adapts to the number of near-ties would gain more.

\paragraph{Recommendation.} If Arena wants its rank spreads to mean that the true ranks lie inside jointly with 95\% probability, pairwise tests with Holm are a simple replacement. They need only the covariance matrix that \texttt{arena-rank} already computes, they widen the 2024 rank spreads by 3.5 ranks on average, and they keep the top three models at single ranks; if a proven guarantee is required, one-sided Holm costs 1.1 ranks more. If the current spreads are kept, the site should state that they are not joint statements and that each one holds with well over 95\% probability on its own.

\paragraph{Limitations and future work.} The simulation assumes independent battles, an exact BT model, fixed strengths and one tie rate; correlated votes or drifting strengths would likely lower real coverage. The true strengths are themselves estimates, slightly more spread out than the real ones, which flatters the simulated coverage. The simulation uses one world of 129 models with 2024 strengths, whereas today's board has about 400 models (Bonferroni intervals would need $z = 3.84$, pairwise Bonferroni $z = 4.98$). All guarantees rely on the normal approximation, and Holm's directional control rests on simulation. Q3's null ignores correlated votes and anything else that varies by category, such as drift combined with a changing category mix. A bootstrap over users or days would calibrate Q3's noise level and Q1's coverage under correlated votes, and synthetic worlds with 200--400 models would show how the conclusions scale. Appendix~\ref{app:limits} lists further limitations and the parts of the proposal we did not carry out.

\subsection{Implementation details and reproducibility}\label{app:impl}

\paragraph{Code and data.} All code, results and figures are at \url{https://github.com/chenxige0509/stat236-arena-uncertainty}. The README lists one command per step, and each script states its inputs, outputs and the numbers it establishes. The battle dump (2.1 GB) is not in the repository; \texttt{prepare\_data.py} converts it to Parquet. The official snapshot is downloaded automatically and read with an allow-list unpickler.

\paragraph{Estimator.} We use \texttt{arena-rank} 0.1.1 with its defaults. \texttt{BradleyTerry} minimizes \eqref{eq:loss} by \mbox{L-BFGS} from zero (at most 1,000 iterations, tolerances $10^{-9}$). The Hessian has a ridge of $10^{-5}$ times the number of battles on its diagonal, which also fixes the location of the strengths, and the intervals use the sandwich covariance with $z = 1.96$. The package returns only the diagonal of $\hat V$; we recompute the full matrix from the same Hessian and gradient covariance. Other software: Python 3.12--3.13, JAX 0.8.1, NumPy 2.3.3, pandas 3.0.6, SciPy 1.18.1, statsmodels 0.15.0.

\paragraph{Simulation.} Each dataset is drawn as multinomial win/tie/loss counts per pair, which is equivalent to drawing the 1.66 million battles one by one, and stored in a fixed-shape array, so the BT fit compiles once and one fit takes about 0.2 seconds. The tie probability is capped at $2\min(p, 1-p)$ so that the probabilities stay valid; the cap binds only for rating gaps above about 270 points and keeps $\mathbb E[y] = p$. A test (\texttt{test\_simulate.py}) checks that this format reproduces \texttt{arena-rank}'s own data loader to $10^{-9}$ and that simulated estimates are unbiased. Q1 and Q2 use seed 0 and identical draws, and \texttt{q2.py} asserts that its Arena rank spreads equal those of Q1. Each Monte Carlo SE is the standard deviation of the per-dataset quantity divided by $\sqrt{1000}$ (for a proportion $\hat\pi$, $\sqrt{\hat\pi(1-\hat\pi)/1000}$). The oracle grid has step 0.05 from 1.8 to 4.0, plus 1.96 and 3.548. The pairwise procedures work on $|z|$ and normal quantiles rather than $p$-values, because thousands of $p$-values underflow; \texttt{test\_pairwise.py} checks them against \texttt{statsmodels} on 200 random correlated problems. Q3 uses seed 0 for the 500 null draws and seed 1 for the power grid, whose critical values and size come from two further sets of 400 fresh null draws. A planted specialty $\delta_{mc}$ shifts model $m$'s strength in every battle of category $c$ by $\mu\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_{mc}}/I_{mc}$, where $I_{mc} = \sum p(1-p)$ and $\mathrm{Var}_{mc} = \sum[p(1-p) - t/4]$ over the pair's battles; at $r = 1.5$ the realized mean $|z^*|$ after the refit is $0.91\mu$. Runtimes on a laptop (Apple silicon): Q1 3 minutes, Q2 4 minutes per world, Q3's calibration 2 minutes, Q3's power grid 35 minutes.

\subsection{Further limitations and deviations from the proposal}\label{app:limits}

\begin{itemize}
\item The truth $\beta^*$ is itself an estimate and is slightly more spread out than the real strengths, which makes ranking a little easier in the simulation than in reality. The stress tests change the strengths but not the comparison graph or the number of models.
\item The normal approximation is weakest for models with few battles; the smallest model in the 2024 data has 1,193 battles.
\item In Q3 the overall fit absorbs part of each specialty, most in the largest categories (median null SD 0.81 for instruction following, which contains 35\% of battles, against 0.95 for Chinese); an overall fit that leaves the category out would avoid this. An empirical null \citep{efron2004large} would be a principled alternative to the bootstrap null.
\item BH's FDR guarantee assumes positive dependence, which the $z^*$ need not satisfy; the simulated false discovery proportions (at most 12\%) are our evidence that it holds approximately.
\item Not carried out from the proposal: bootstrap rank intervals as a marginal benchmark; BH on the pairwise tests; fully synthetic worlds with other comparison graphs and numbers of models; and a low-rank-plus-sparse decomposition that removes a general-ability component before testing for specialties. We also replaced the proposal's 2025 data sample by the 2024 dump, for the answer key described in \cref{sec:data}.
\end{itemize}
