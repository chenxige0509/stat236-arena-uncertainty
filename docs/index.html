Chatbot Arena · Bradley–Terry · multiple testing

How certain is the Chatbot Arena leaderboard?

Arena ranks language models from millions of head-to-head votes and prints a range next to each one, such as rank 2–10. That range is built from per-model 95% intervals with no correction for comparing 129 models at once. We measured how often it is right, which corrections fix it, and what they cost.

Step 0 · What the site computes

A rank spread is read off overlapping score intervals

Each model gets a Bradley–Terry rating and a 95% interval. Its best rank is one plus the number of models whose whole interval sits above its own; its worst rank is one plus the number whose interval reaches above its lower end. We refit the public 2024 battle data with Arena's own code and matched the published ratings to a median of 0.06 points. On today's site every interval on all 4,773 published 2026 text boards is exactly the rating ± 1.96 standard errors, and the formula reproduces both ends of the displayed spread for all 59 models we recorded.

The original Arena paper says a ranking needs intervals that hold simultaneously. The site's intervals hold one model at a time. That creates two errors that pull in opposite directions.

best  = 1 + #{ models with L > my U }
worst = 1 + #{ others with U > my L }
−

Too narrow. 5% of 95% intervals miss. With 129 models, about 6.7 miss in a typical dataset, and all 129 hold in almost none.

+

Too cautious. Two models are separated only if their intervals do not touch. For two independent estimates with equal standard errors, that is a test of their difference at |z| > 1.96·√2 = 2.77, a two-sided level of 0.6% instead of 5%.

Q1 · Diagnosis

How often is the whole table of rank spreads right?

In 90.9% of simulated leaderboards, a little short of the 95% target. The two errors above almost cancel.

We cannot check real ranks against the truth, so we built a world where the truth is known. Each square below is one simulated leaderboard.

Nothing in Arena's method targets the 90.9%; it depends on how many models happen to be nearly tied (see the stress test in Q2). Which ranks are shaky? Crowding matters most: the number of models within 10 rating points of a model has rank correlation 0.79 with the width of its spread. Strength itself barely matters once crowding and the standard error are accounted for.

Q2 · Repair

Which correction fixes the spreads, and what does it cost?

Testing each pair of models directly, with Holm's step-down correction. Its spreads held in all 1,000 datasets at a mean width of 11.3 ranks, against 13.6 for Bonferroni and 33–43 for the χ² set of the original Arena paper.

First, how wide do the spreads need to be? Arena's intervals are rating ± z × standard error with z = 1.96. Drag the multiplier, or use the three buttons, and see what the whole leaderboard does across the same 1,000 simulated datasets.

The smallest z that reaches 95% is about 2.1. That choice uses the true ranks, so no real procedure can make it; it shows how little extra width the table actually needs.

Six valid fixes, very different prices

Every correction below keeps all 129 rank intervals right in all 1,000 datasets. They differ in how much width they spend doing it. The χ² set from the original Arena paper protects every linear combination of the 129 scores, far more than a ranking needs. A ranking only needs the sign of each pairwise difference, so testing each pair of models directly is cheaper, and Holm's step-down procedure is the cheapest.

Holm sorts the 8,256 pairs by |z| of their rating gap. The largest must clear the Bonferroni bar of 4.52; each rejection lowers the bar for the rest, down to 3.96 on average here because thousands of pairs are clear-cut. A one-sided version of Holm, whose control of wrong orderings is fully proven, never goes below 4.38 and needs 12.4 ranks.

Stress test: what happens when models are tied?

The real 2024 strengths have few near-ties, so every correction looks overly safe. We rebuilt the world with tied models, where the guarantees are actually tested. Share of 1,000 datasets in which all rank intervals are right:

Shaded: below the 95% target. Arena's uncorrected spreads collapse once ties exist. Holm stays above 95% everywhere; its guarantee is a theorem for exact ties, and the near-tie world, where no theorem applies, turns out to be easier.

Q3 · Category leaderboards

Are category “specialties” real?

Some clearly are, above all language effects of 50 to 84 rating points. How many are real is less certain: between 275 and 26 of 958 pairs, depending on how much noise the votes carry.

Arena also publishes boards for coding, math, Chinese and more. For each model and category with at least 200 battles we compare the model's score in that category with what its overall strength predicts, and standardize the gap (z*) against 500 simulated boards with no specialties. Higher Criticism, min-P and χ² all reject “no specialties at all” with p = 0.002, the smallest value 500 null draws can give. The open question is how many pairs to flag.

What we learned

Takeaways

Q1

Nearly right by accident

Arena's spreads hold for the whole table 91% of the time because two errors roughly cancel. Nothing in the method targets that number. When models come in groups of exact ties it falls to 7–24%, and to 0.2% when all are tied.

Q2

Test the pairs a ranking needs

A ranking only needs the signs of pairwise differences. Pairwise tests with Holm give valid spreads 18% narrower than Bonferroni and about three to four times narrower than the χ² set.

Real board

223 orderings do not survive

Of the 7,808 “A beats B” statements implied by Arena's intervals, Holm does not support 223 once all 8,256 pairs are counted. In the other direction, Holm separates no pair that Arena shows as tied.

Q3

Specialties exist; counting them is harder

Language effects are large and unmistakable. The number of significant specialties ranges from 275 at our null's noise level down to 26 if the real noise is three times larger.

Limits. The simulation treats battles as independent and strengths as fixed, uses one 2024 snapshot with 129 models (today's board has about 400), and relies on normal approximations. Correlated votes would likely lower Arena's coverage further.

Data: the public LMSYS battle dump of 2024-08-14 (1.66 million battles after Arena's filter), the official 2024-08-12 leaderboard snapshot, and LMArena's 2026 leaderboard-dataset. Code: Arena's open-source arena-rank package plus our simulation, all in the project repository; the report has the methods, proofs and full results.
